Ask a model trained on a small dataset why it made a prediction, then retrain it on a slightly different resample of the same data and ask again. When there are far fewer examples than features, the two explanations can disagree — sometimes on whether a feature pushed the prediction up or down. An explanation that changes every time you look is hard to trust. Bootstrap Wasserstein Alignment (BWA) is a way to turn many unstable explanations into one stable consensus, and to say how sure that consensus is.

## The problem

Feature-attribution methods assign each input feature a share of a model's prediction. In low-data regimes, where the number of samples _N_ is much smaller than the number of features _d_, these attributions become unreliable: they are inconsistent across bootstrap replicates of the training data.

The obvious fix is to average the replicates. The paper shows why that can fail. When a feature's attribution flips sign from one replicate to the next, positive and negative values cancel, and the averaged explanation shrinks towards zero. The paper's Lemma 1 formalises this worst case — the _norm collapse_ of Euclidean averaging under sign-flip symmetry — and it motivates a geometric approach instead.

## Method

BWA treats each replicate's explanation as a geometric object rather than a list of numbers to average:

1. **Project to the simplex.** Each bootstrap replicate's attribution is projected to a probability measure on the feature simplex — a distribution of importance over the features.
2. **Find the consensus.** The replicates are aggregated with a **Wasserstein-barycenter** consensus: the distribution that is closest, in optimal-transport distance, to all of them.
3. **Vote on signs, and abstain.** A per-feature sign-abstention mechanism decides each feature's direction, and declines to assign one when the replicates disagree.

For reference, the standard definition of an entropic Wasserstein barycenter of distributions <Math tex="\nu_1,\dots,\nu_K" /> with weights <Math tex="\lambda_k" /> is

<Math display tex="\bar\mu = \arg\min_{\mu} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \lambda_k \, W_\varepsilon(\mu, \nu_k)" />

where <Math tex="W_\varepsilon" /> is the entropy-regularised optimal-transport cost.

The paper presents two versions:

- **BWA-v1** — a hard binomial sign test with global normalisation.
- **BWA-v2** — a magnitude-weighted soft sign <Math tex="s_j = \sum_b e_j^{(b)} / \sum_b |e_j^{(b)}|" /> times a per-feature median absolute-value scale, which keeps natural attribution magnitudes.

The paper is explicit about what the barycenter does in each version. In **BWA-v1** it carries the consensus. In **BWA-v2** it does not: the paper proves that v2 is an exact positive per-feature rescaling of the bootstrap mean, so its signs are the mean’s signs and the cost matrix has no effect — checked numerically with zero sign disagreements across 8,465 features. v2 closes v1’s sign-accuracy gap *by construction*, and the barycenter remains in v2 only as an uncertainty diagnostic. The animation above shows the v1 pipeline.

BWA also reports how reliable each explanation is: a bootstrap-level uncertainty <Math tex="\sigma_{UQ}" /> (the variance from retraining the model) and a continuous per-feature confidence from the soft sign.

## What I found

The evaluation spans eight experiments: synthetic data (_N_ = 20, _d_ = 100, _B_ = 50 bootstrap replicates, 100 trials), MNIST saliency (_N_ = 100, _d_ = 784, 5 seeds × 50 images), Golub leukemia gene expression (_N_ = 72, _d_ ∈ {500, 2000}) and four UCI tabular datasets (_N_ ∈ {20, 50}).

Across them, BWA-v2 closes BWA-v1's documented sign-accuracy gap by 5–15 percentage points. Where the true signal is sparse — images and gene expression — BWA-v2's attribution maps are significantly **sparser** than every Euclidean baseline. That is the real gain: sparser explanations, not more accurate or more stable ones (see below). On dense tabular data it ties the simple average.

## What was hard

- **Finding out what the method really does.** The revised paper proves that BWA-v2’s sign is exactly the bootstrap mean’s sign and that its cost matrix is inert — so the Wasserstein machinery, the paper’s namesake, matters for v1 but not for v2. It says so plainly instead of crediting optimal transport for v2’s gains.
- **Keeping figures and numbers consistent.** The revision found a gene-expression figure that did not match its caption, a metric labelled Jaccard that was really precision, and tables reporting a different run than the abstract. Each was traced to its cause and corrected.
- **The solver’s cliff.** v1’s Sinkhorn barycenter never converges below ε = 0.05 and always converges above it, so the regularisation is not a free choice.
- **A major revision.** The paper went through a full major revision with two reviewers, including new experiments (an ablation and run-to-run stability) before acceptance.

## What it did not show

- **Not more stable.** Plain averaging is marginally but consistently *more* stable run to run than BWA-v2 on every measure tested (cosine, norm variation, Spearman, top-10 overlap). Readers who want stability rather than sparsity should use the mean.
- **Not more accurate.** Sign accuracy is the bootstrap mean’s by construction, and no method — BWA included — separates on top-10 feature recovery.
- **No gain on dense data.** On tabular datasets where many features matter, BWA-v2 ties the simple average on every metric.
